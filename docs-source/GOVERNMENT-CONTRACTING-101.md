## Part 1 — The Contract Vehicles: How the Government Buys

### 1.1 The three lanes

Before the acronyms, understand that there are three fundamentally different ways the government can pay for something:

| Lane | What it is | Best analogy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Grant** | Free money to fund research, usually with no product delivered to the government | A scholarship |
| **Contract** | The government buys a product or service from you, by the rules of the FAR | A purchase order |
| **Other Transaction (OT)** | A flexible agreement for research or prototypes that bypasses most FAR rules | A startup partnership |

Almost every acronym in this guide lives in one of these lanes. SBIR and STTR are **grant-and-contract hybrids** (technically awards, often structured as contracts). OTA and CSO are **other transactions**. RFI and RFP are **contracting tools**.

### 1.2 SBIR — Small Business Innovation Research

**What it is.** A congressionally mandated program (run by the Small Business Administration, or SBA) that sets aside a percentage of federal R&D budgets for small businesses. Eleven agencies participate — DoD, NASA, NIH, NSF, DHS, DOE, and others — and each publishes its own solicitations.

**Who qualifies.** A U.S.-owned, for-profit small business with 500 or fewer employees. The company does the work in the United States.

**How it works — the phases.**

| Phase | What happens | Typical size | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Phase I** | Feasibility: prove the idea works | ~$50K–$325K (caps vary by agency and year) | 3–12 months |
| **Phase II** | Build the prototype / finish the R&D | ~$750K–$2.2M (caps vary) | 15–24 months |
| **Phase III** | Commercialize — no SBIR money | Private or non-SBIR funds | Ongoing |

Phase III is the important one for the business: it's the *reason* to do SBIR. Phase I and II are taxpayer-funded R&D that de-risks your technology; Phase III is where you sell the resulting product to the government or the commercial market — often without competition.

**Why companies like it.** The money is non-dilutive (you don't give up equity), the program is structured and fair, and a Phase I award is a credible credential that makes everything else easier — customers, investors, partners.

**The catch.** It's competitive and it's a long game. Most agencies have open-topic solicitations; you write a proposal describing the problem, your approach, and the commercial payoff. Proposal Genie's **SBIR Grant Proposal** template in the Writing Studio exists for exactly this.

### 1.3 STTR — Small Business Technology Transfer

**What it is.** SBIR's sibling, with one crucial difference: **the small business must team with a nonprofit research institution** — a university, a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC), or a qualified nonprofit.

**The mandatory split.** For STTR, the small business must perform **at least 40%** of the R&D work, and the research partner must perform **at least 30%**. The small business is the prime (the one who signs the agreement).

**How it differs from SBIR, in one table:**

| | SBIR | STTR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agencies | 11 | 5 (DoD, NASA, NIH, NSF, DOE) |
| Research partner | Not required | **Required** (university/nonprofit) |
| Work split | No partner to split with | ≥40% small business, ≥30% research institution |
| Best for | A company with its own R&D muscle | A company that needs academic science |

**The strategy.** STTR is how a small company gets access to university labs, professors, and cutting-edge science without paying for it. If your technology depends on research you don't own, STTR is the vehicle that funds the partnership.

### 1.4 OTA — Other Transaction Authority

**What it is.** A legal authority (for DoD, 10 U.S.C. § 4021 for research and § 4022 for prototypes) that lets an agency do a deal **outside the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR)**. OTAs are not procurement contracts and not grants — they're "other transactions," with the flexibility to negotiate terms that a FAR contract can't have.

**Why it exists.** The FAR was built for buying commodities predictably. It's slow for cutting-edge work. OTAs let agencies move fast with innovative companies — the Defense Innovation Unit (DIU), for example, runs almost entirely on prototype OTAs.

**How it works.** An agency issues an **opportunity announcement** (often called a "prototype project opportunity"). Companies respond with whitepapers or proposals. The agency picks winners and signs a prototype OTA. If the prototype works, the agency can award **follow-on production** — sometimes without a new competition.

**What it means for you.** OTA work is often easier to win than a full RFP (less red tape, faster decisions, smaller teams), and the companies that win prototypes are strongly positioned for the production work that follows. The trade: fewer published rules, so capture and relationships matter more.

### 1.5 CSO — Commercial Solutions Opening

**What it is.** A competitive process (for DoD, 10 U.S.C. § 3458) that lets agencies buy **innovative commercial solutions** — products or services that already exist or nearly exist in the commercial market — using a streamlined, whitepaper-based approach.

**How it works.**

1. The agency publishes a **CSO notice** describing the problem it wants solved.
2. Industry submits short **whitepapers** (no full proposals yet).
3. The agency selects the most promising and invites them to submit full proposals.
4. Winners can receive a prototype OTA or a FAR-based contract.

**Why it matters.** CSO is the commercial-first cousin of OTA. It's the vehicle agencies use when they believe the answer already exists in the commercial market — your product — rather than something that needs to be invented. If your company sells a commercial product with a defense or federal application, CSO is the door.

### 1.6 RFI — Request for Information

**What it is.** The government saying: *"We're thinking about buying something. Tell us what exists."*

An RFI is **market research, not a competition**. Responding does not lead directly to a contract, and not responding doesn't disqualify you from anything. Agencies publish RFIs to learn what's available, who the vendors are, and how to shape a future solicitation.

**Why you should respond anyway.** Three reasons:

1. **You shape the requirement.** The agencies read the responses, and good RFI answers influence what the eventual RFP asks for.
2. **You get on their radar.** Agencies keep lists of interested vendors for market research and outreach.
3. **You're invited to the party.** Many RFIs lead to one-on-one meetings or industry days that feed directly into capture.

### 1.7 RFP — Request for Proposal

**What it is.** The classic, formal competition. The agency has a real requirement, real money, and a published evaluation plan (Section M), and it asks industry for **complete proposals**: technical approach, management plan, past performance, and price.

**The rules of the game.** RFPs are governed by FAR Part 15. The agency announces it on SAM.gov, companies ask written questions, submit by the deadline, and the agency scores every proposal against the published criteria. Winners are chosen by **best value** (technical + past performance + price weighed together) or **LPTA** (lowest price technically acceptable).

**This is Proposal Genie's home turf.** The Procurements tab, Dissect RFP, the compliance matrix, the Proposal Studio — all of it is built around the RFP. Everything in *Win With Their Words* is about winning RFPs.

### 1.8 The quick-reference table

| Vehicle | Full name | What it is | Leads to a contract? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SBIR** | Small Business Innovation Research | Phased R&D funding for small businesses | Phase III (commercial, non-SBIR) |
| **STTR** | Small Business Technology Transfer | SBIR with a required research partner | Phase III (commercial, non-SBIR) |
| **OTA** | Other Transaction Authority | Flexible R&D/prototype agreement outside the FAR | Prototype → possible production |
| **CSO** | Commercial Solutions Opening | Whitepaper competition for commercial solutions | Prototype OTA or contract |
| **RFI** | Request for Information | Market research; not a solicitation | No (but shapes future ones) |
| **RFP** | Request for Proposal | Full competition for complete solutions | Yes — the big one |

**Also in the alphabet soup** (you'll meet these too):

| Acronym | Full name | One-liner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **RFQ** | Request for Quotation | Price-focused small buy |
| **IFB** | Invitation for Bid | Lowest acceptable price wins |
| **BAA** | Broad Agency Announcement | Open-ended research interests |
| **IDIQ** | Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity | A pre-negotiated pool of work you task against |
| **GSA Schedule** | (MAS) | Pre-negotiated catalog contract for commercial items |

### 1.9 Which lane should your company chase?

- **R&D company, no university ties?** → SBIR
- **R&D company that needs academic science?** → STTR
- **Commercial product with a federal use?** → CSO, or GSA Schedule
- **Innovative capability for DoD/NASA/DHS?** → OTA (watch DIU, SSC Front Door, SpaceWERX, AFWERX)
- **Established service provider?** → RFP (and IDIQ vehicles)
- **New to all of it?** → Start with SAM.gov registration (Part 2) and respond to RFIs while you learn

## Part 2 — SAM.gov: The Front Door

### 2.1 What SAM.gov is

**SAM** stands for **System for Award Management**. It is the federal government's single, official website (run by the General Services Administration, GSA) for doing business with the government. It does several jobs in one place:

1. **Entity registration** — your company's official federal profile. **You must be registered to receive a federal contract or grant.**
2. **Contract opportunities** — the official database where agencies post solicitations. **Every federal contract opportunity over $25,000 must be posted here.**
3. **Contract data** — awarded contracts and spending (searchable, and connected to USASpending.gov).
4. **Entity exclusions** — the list of companies barred from doing business with the government.
5. **Wage determinations** — prevailing wage data for service contracts.

In short: SAM.gov is where you register as a vendor, and where you search for the work. Both halves matter for Proposal Genie — registration is your company's federal identity, and the opportunities database is what the **Opportunity Scout** searches.

### 2.2 What registration gives you

When you complete your SAM.gov registration, you get (or confirm) three identifiers that follow your company for its entire federal career:

- **UEI (Unique Entity ID)** — your company's 12-character federal ID, issued free at SAM.gov. (It replaced the old DUNS number in April 2022.)
- **CAGE code** — a 5-character code assigned by the Department of Defense that identifies your company in federal systems. (More in Part 3.)
- **Reps & Certs** — your annual representations and certifications: your NAICS codes, your small business status, your socio-economic classifications, and a list of legal certifications.

### 2.3 Registration, step by step

The whole process takes about **1–3 hours of your time** (gathering documents) plus **processing time** (usually ~3 business days, up to 10–15 if there are reviews). Do it early — never the week a proposal is due.

**Step 1 — Create a Login.gov account.**
Go to [login.gov](https://login.gov) and create the federal identity account you'll use to log in to SAM.gov. You'll need email, a phone, and to set up two-factor authentication.

**Step 2 — Go to SAM.gov and start a registration.**
Go to [sam.gov](https://sam.gov) → **Register Entity**. You'll log in with your Login.gov account.

**Step 3 — Get your UEI.**
SAM.gov will ask if you already have a UEI. If you don't, you'll get one during registration — it's free and instant. (Be careful: third-party sites sometimes charge for this. The UEI is free at SAM.gov.)

**Step 4 — Complete the entity profile.**
This is the long form. Have ready:

- **Legal business name** — exactly as it appears on your incorporation/formation documents and your IRS records (this must match your EIN)
- **Physical address** — not a PO box (this is where the government mails you and where the CAGE code is tied)
- **Taxpayer ID (EIN)** — your IRS Employer Identification Number
- **Your NAICS codes** — the industry codes for what you do (Part 3)
- **Your socio-economic statuses** — small business, 8(a), HUBZone, WOSB, SDVOSB, and so on (Part 4)
- **Banking information** — for receiving payment via EFT (electronic funds transfer), including the ABA routing number and account number

**Step 5 — Complete the representations and certifications.**
Answer the reps & certs accurately. This is a legal declaration — get it right, and keep it current.

**Step 6 — Submit and track.**
Submit the registration, then use the SAM.gov status tracker. Typical processing is about **3 business days**; allow up to **10–15** if your registration needs external review (for example, if your legal name doesn't match your IRS records).

**Step 7 — Mark your calendar for renewal.**
Your registration expires **365 days after approval**. There is no automatic renewal. If it lapses, you cannot receive awards until it's active again — and your reps & certs become stale. Set an annual reminder 60 days before your anniversary.

### 2.4 The rules in one box

- **It's free.** SAM.gov registration costs nothing. Never pay a third-party site that claims to register you "for a fee" — the only official site is sam.gov.
- **It expires annually.** Renew every 365 days, or lose eligibility.
- **It's required.** No active SAM.gov registration = no federal contract, no grant, no OTA.
- **Keep it current.** A name change, address change, or new ownership means updating the registration.

### 2.5 Scams to avoid

- Sites that charge for "SAM registration" or "UEI registration" — both are free at the official sites.
- "Renewal reminder" services that bill you to renew what you can renew free.
- Emails that ask for your banking details or Login.gov password. SAM.gov communicates through your account; it never asks for your password by email.

## Part 3 — The Numbers and Codes That Identify You

### 3.1 NAICS codes — what industry you're in

**NAICS** stands for the **North American Industry Classification System** — the standard the U.S., Canada, and Mexico use to classify businesses by what they do. It replaced the old SIC system in 1997 and is revised every five years (the current edition is 2022).

**The structure.** A NAICS code has six digits, and each digit narrows the industry:

| Digits | Level | Example (541511) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Sector | 54 — Professional, Scientific & Technical Services |
| 3 | Subsector | 541 — Professional, Scientific & Technical Services |
| 4 | Industry group | 5415 — Computer Systems Design |
| 5 | NAICS industry | 54151 — Computer Systems Design and Related Services |
| 6 | National industry | 541511 — Custom Computer Programming Services |

**Why NAICS codes matter to you (three reasons):**

1. **They're how you're found.** Agencies classify every solicitation by NAICS, and searches (including Proposal Genie's Scout) match your codes against theirs. Choose codes that describe what you actually do.
2. **They determine your size.** The SBA's small business **size standard** — the revenue or headcount cutoff that decides whether you're a "small business" — is set per NAICS code. More in Part 4.
3. **They're on every solicitation.** The contracting officer assigns a NAICS code to each buy, and that code determines which businesses are eligible (e.g., whether it can be set aside for small business).

**How to choose yours.** In SAM.gov, you select the NAICS codes that match your **primary business activity**. Most companies list a handful — their core code plus adjacent ones. Good examples in this space: **541511** (custom computer programming), **541330** (engineering services), **334118** (computer terminal manufacturing), **541715** (R&D in physical sciences). Look up codes at census.gov/naics, and mirror the codes you see on solicitations you'd want to win.

### 3.2 CAGE codes — who you are to the DoD

**CAGE** stands for **Commercial and Government Entity**. It's a 5-character alphanumeric code (e.g., `3A7K2`) that identifies your company in federal systems — and in NATO countries, where it's called an **NCAGE**.

**Who issues it.** The Defense Logistics Agency (DLA). For U.S. companies, the CAGE code is **assigned automatically during SAM.gov registration** — you don't apply for it separately.

**Why it matters.** Your CAGE code appears on solicitations, proposals, and awards; it's how the government's purchasing systems know who you are. You'll need it when you register on agency portals (like DIU or SBIR.gov), when you order from federal catalogs, and on essentially every document you submit.

**One rule to remember:** your CAGE code is tied to your **physical address**. Move addresses and you may need a new one.

### 3.3 UEI — the ID that replaced DUNS

The **Unique Entity ID** is a 12-character identifier (e.g., `K5Q7XL9J2M31`) issued free by SAM.gov. Before April 2022 the government used DUNS numbers from a private company; the UEI brought the identifier in-house, made it free, and made it instant. You get your UEI during SAM.gov registration, keep it for life (it doesn't change when you renew), and you'll enter it on nearly every federal form. The short version: **UEI = who you are to the federal government; CAGE = who you are to the DoD; NAICS = what industry you're in.**

## Part 4 — Economic Classifications of Companies

When the government asks about your company's "economic classification," it's asking two different questions:

1. **What kind of legal entity are you?** (structure)
2. **Do you qualify for small business programs?** (size and socio-economic status)

Both are part of your SAM.gov profile, and both decide what you're eligible to compete for.

### 4.1 Legal structure

Your entity type is the first classification:

- **Sole proprietorship** — you personally; no separate company
- **Partnership** — two or more owners
- **LLC (Limited Liability Company)** — flexible, pass-through taxation
- **S-Corporation** — pass-through taxation with corporate formalities
- **C-Corporation** — a full tax-paying corporation (common for VC-backed tech)
- **Nonprofit / 501(c)(3)** — eligible for grants, not most set-asides

For most government work, an LLC or S-corp is the standard choice. The key practical point: **the legal name and EIN you register in SAM.gov must match your formation and IRS documents exactly** — mismatches are the #1 cause of registration delays.

### 4.2 Small business status and size standards

The SBA defines "small business" industry by industry, using **size standards** — a revenue cap (for service businesses) or an employee cap (for manufacturers) tied to each NAICS code. Examples:

| NAICS | Industry | Size standard (2022 edition) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 541511 | Custom computer programming | $34M average annual receipts |
| 541330 | Engineering services | $25.5M |
| 334118 | Computer terminal manufacturing | 1,250 employees |
| 541715 | R&D in physical sciences | 1,000 employees |

Size standards change with each NAICS revision, so always check the current SBA table (sba.gov/size-standards). **You self-certify** your small business status in SAM.gov (and for most categories, in the SBA's certification systems). If you qualify as small, you become eligible for **set-asides** — competitions where only small businesses may bid.

### 4.3 Socio-economic classifications

Beyond "small," the government recognizes specific categories of small businesses, each with its own program and its own advantages:

| Classification | Who qualifies | What it gets you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **8(a) Business Development** | Socially and economically disadvantaged owners; 9-year program | Sole-source authority and set-asides; SBA mentoring |
| **SDB** — Small Disadvantaged Business | Economically disadvantaged small business | Set-aside eligibility; evaluation credit |
| **HUBZone** | Business in a historically underutilized business zone, with employees living there | HUBZone set-asides; 10% evaluation preference |
| **WOSB** — Women-Owned Small Business | Majority women-owned and -controlled | Set-asides in specific NAICS codes |
| **EDWOSB** — Economically Disadvantaged WOSB | WOSB that is also economically disadvantaged | WOSB set-asides (priority tier) |
| **VOSB** — Veteran-Owned Small Business | Majority veteran-owned | VA set-aside eligibility |
| **SDVOSB** — Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned | Majority service-disabled veteran-owned | Sole-source and set-aside authority (especially VA) |

**Why this matters more than almost anything else in this guide.** Set-asides are competitions with *fewer competitors* — often much fewer. An 8(a) or SDVOSB set-aside might attract a handful of bidders where the open competition attracts hundreds. For a small company, the single most powerful thing you can do in year one is **claim every classification you honestly qualify for**.

**Where you certify.** Some statuses are self-certified in SAM.gov (small business, WOSB, VOSB, SDB). Others require certification: **8(a)** and **HUBZone** are certified through the SBA (certify.sba.gov), and **SDVOSB/VOSB** for VA work are certified through the VA's **VetBiz** system. The SBA has been consolidating certification into its certify.sba.gov portal — check the current requirements.

### 4.4 Putting the classifications together

A complete company profile in SAM.gov reads something like:

> Acme Engineering LLC — S-Corporation · NAICS 541330, 541511 · CAGE 3A7K2 · UEI K5Q7XL9J2M31 · Small Business (25.5M standard) · HUBZone certified · WOSB

Every element of that sentence is a lever. The legal structure makes you eligible. The NAICS codes determine your size standard and what you can search for. The UEI and CAGE make you findable and payable. The socio-economic classifications open the doors with fewer competitors. Together they determine which of the vehicles from Part 1 you can pursue.

## Part 5 — Putting It All Together

A quick walkthrough of a realistic first year, tying the parts together:

**Month 1 — Register.**
Create a Login.gov account, complete your SAM.gov registration with your UEI, CAGE, NAICS codes, and banking info, and self-certify as a small business. (Part 2.)

**Month 2 — Classify.**
Check the current SBA size standard for your primary NAICS. If you qualify for HUBZone, 8(a), WOSB, or SDVOSB, start those certifications now — they take months, and they're the single biggest leverage you'll ever get. (Part 4.)

**Month 3 — Search.**
Start watching SAM.gov for opportunities matching your NAICS codes. Set up Proposal Genie's **Opportunity Scout** with your company profile, NAICS codes, and target agencies — it does the watching for you, and it scores every opportunity against your capabilities.

**Month 4+ — Respond and learn.**
Answer RFIs (you shape the future requirement). Pursue SBIR or STTR if your company is R&D-oriented. Track OTAs and CSOs from DIU, SpaceWERX, and the other innovation offices. When an RFP lands, run it through the full Proposal Genie pipeline — dissect it, build the compliance matrix, write the NLP way, and verify everything before you submit.

And whatever you win or lose, record the outcome. The loop — register, classify, search, respond, learn — is the whole game, and it compounds.

## Quick Glossary

- **8(a)** — SBA program for socially and economically disadvantaged small businesses; sole-source authority
- **BAA** — Broad Agency Announcement; open-ended research interests
- **CAGE code** — 5-character DoD identifier for your company (assigned during SAM registration)
- **CSO** — Commercial Solutions Opening; whitepaper competition for commercial solutions
- **DUNS** — the old federal identifier, replaced by the UEI in 2022
- **FAR** — Federal Acquisition Regulation; the rulebook for federal buying
- **GSA Schedule (MAS)** — pre-negotiated catalog contracts for commercial items
- **HUBZone** — set-aside program for businesses in historically underutilized zones
- **IDIQ** — Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity; a task-order pool
- **NAICS** — 6-digit industry classification; drives searches, size standards, and set-asides
- **OTA** — Other Transaction Authority; flexible R&D/prototype agreements outside the FAR
- **RFI** — Request for Information; market research
- **RFP** — Request for Proposal; the full competition
- **SAM.gov** — System for Award Management; the federal registry and opportunities database
- **SBIR** — Small Business Innovation Research; phased R&D funding for small businesses
- **SDB** — Small Disadvantaged Business
- **SDVOSB** — Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business
- **Set-aside** — a competition reserved for a class of businesses (small, 8(a), HUBZone, WOSB, SDVOSB)
- **STTR** — Small Business Technology Transfer; SBIR with a required research partner
- **UEI** — Unique Entity ID; your free 12-character federal identifier
- **WOSB / EDWOSB** — Women-Owned Small Business / Economically Disadvantaged WOSB
