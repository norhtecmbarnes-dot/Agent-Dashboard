## About This Book

This book is the companion guide to **Proposal Genie**, the local-first proposal studio that lives inside the PersonalAI Dashboard. It was written for people who are **completely new** to selling to the government — no procurement experience needed.

Two things make this book different from other "how to win contracts" guides:

1. **It is very basic.** We explain everything. Every acronym, every weird word like *compliance matrix* or *quad chart*, is unpacked in plain English the first time you meet it. There is also a full glossary at the back, so you can always look a term up.

2. **It has one big idea that shapes how you write.** The best proposals do not sound like the company that wrote them. They sound like the **customer**. We call this the *NLP way* — Neuro-Linguistic Programming — and the simplest version of it is this:

> **Repeat the customer's words back to them.**

If the agency writes "we need resilient, survivable space capabilities," your proposal should use the words *resilient* and *survivable* and *space capabilities* — not "robust satellite systems." When evaluators read your proposal and keep hearing their own mission, their own terms, their own priorities, they feel *understood*. And people give contracts to the company that understands them.

This book teaches you the government buying process, walks you through the Proposal Genie workflow, and shows you — with real before-and-after examples — how to write the NLP way.

## Part 1 — How the Government Buys (The Basics)

### 1.1 The government is a customer

It is easy to think of "the government" as a giant, scary machine. Let's shrink it. The government is just a **customer** — a customer with a mission (protect the country, map the oceans, cure disease, explore space) that needs **products and services** (software, satellites, training, research, IT support) and has a legal obligation to buy them **fairly** and **transparently**.

That last part matters. A normal company can pick any vendor it likes. The government **cannot** — the law (mostly the *Federal Acquisition Regulation*, or **FAR**) says it must announce what it needs, let everyone compete, score everyone by the same rules, and pick the best value. That rule is your opportunity: if you can compete fairly and answer well, you can win.

### 1.2 How a purchase becomes a contract

Every government buy follows the same basic rhythm:

1. **Need.** An agency realizes it needs something.
2. **Advertise.** It publishes an announcement (most commonly on **SAM.gov**) describing the need and the rules.
3. **Compete.** Companies study the announcement, ask questions, and submit proposals.
4. **Evaluate.** The agency scores every proposal against published criteria.
5. **Award.** The winner gets the contract. The losers can ask *why* (a **debrief**).

That's the whole machine. Everything else — every acronym in this book — is detail around those five steps.

### 1.3 The solicitation family: RFI, RFQ, RFP, IFB

The announcement the government publishes is called a **solicitation** — an invitation to compete. There are several kinds, and it is worth knowing which one you are looking at:

| Type | Full name | What it means | Does it lead to a contract? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **RFI** | Request for Information | "We're thinking about buying something. Tell us what exists." A market research tool. | No — it's research. |
| **RFQ** | Request for Quotation | "We know exactly what we need and it's mostly about price." | Yes, but small and price-driven. |
| **RFP** | Request for Proposal | "We need a complete solution — your approach, your experience, your price." | Yes — this is the big one. |
| **IFB** | Invitation for Bid | "We need the lowest acceptable price. Period." (Also called a sealed bid.) | Yes, lowest price wins. |

Most of the work in Proposal Genie happens with **RFPs** — complete solutions judged on more than price.

### 1.4 The RFP lifecycle — and where Proposal Genie fits

Experienced capture professionals say **most contracts are won before the RFP is even published** — in the research and relationship phase. The full lifecycle looks like this:

**Phase 1 — Pre-solicitation (capture).** The agency does market research and talks to industry. Smart companies identify the opportunity early, learn the customer, and shape the requirements. *This is where the Proposal Genie **Scout** and **Customers** tools live.*

**Phase 2 — Solicitation release and questions.** The RFP appears on SAM.gov. Companies read it, ask written questions, and decide whether to bid (**go/no-go**). *This is where Proposal Genie's **Procurements** tab and **Dissect RFP** button live.*

**Phase 3 — Proposal development.** Companies write the response: technical approach, management plan, past performance, price. *This is the **Proposal Studio**.*

**Phase 4 — Submission and compliance check.** The agency checks that every rule was followed — font, page limits, forms, deadline. Miss one rule and you can be eliminated *before anyone reads your ideas*. *This is the **compliance matrix**.*

**Phase 5 — Evaluation.** Evaluators score each proposal against the published criteria. *This is where **scoring** and **win themes** pay off.*

**Phase 6 — Award and after.** The winner is announced. Losers can request a **debrief** — a meeting where the agency explains why they won or lost. *This feeds back into Proposal Genie's **Customers** knowledge base so the next proposal is smarter.*

### 1.5 Who's who on the government side

- **Contracting Officer (CO)** — the only person legally allowed to sign the contract. The referee of the process.
- **Program Manager (PM)** — the person who will actually run the work after award. Their pain is your gold: they know the real problems.
- **Evaluators** — the people who read and score your proposal. They use only what the RFP says (Section M) plus what you wrote.
- **Source Selection Authority (SSA)** — the senior official who makes the final award decision.

### 1.6 Section L and Section M — the two most important pages

Every federal RFP contains two sections that tell you everything:

- **Section L — Instructions to Offerors.** *How* to submit: what volumes to write, how many pages, what font, what forms, the deadline. These are rules. Break one and you risk elimination.
- **Section M — Evaluation Factors for Award.** *How* you will be scored: the criteria, their weights, and how the winner is chosen.

Proposal Genie's **dissection** feature reads the RFP and extracts exactly these things — compliance requirements (Section L), scoring factors with weights (Section M), milestones, and format rules — so you never have to guess.

### 1.7 Where opportunities are found

- **SAM.gov** — the official federal database of contract opportunities (all contracts over $25,000 must be posted here). Also where you register your business (the **System for Award Management** registration).
- **Agency forecasts** — many agencies publish their planned purchases a year ahead. Gold for capture planning.
- **USASpending.gov** — what the government has actually bought, and from whom. Great for seeing your competitors.
- **Set-asides** — contracts reserved for small businesses, including special categories like **8(a)**, **HUBZone**, **WOSB** (women-owned), and **SDVOSB** (service-disabled veteran-owned). Fewer competitors, better odds.

## Part 2 — The Proposal Genie Workflow

Now that you know how the government buys, here is how Proposal Genie walks you through it, step by step.

### 2.1 The Corporate Vault (your company's memory)

Before anything else, Proposal Genie needs to know **your company**. The **Corporate Vault** is a private knowledge base that stores:

- Company documents: past proposals, product sheets, capability statements, your **brand voice** (the way your company talks)
- Your **Company** profile and a **soul** file that defines how the AI should behave
- Everything your company has learned

Think of it as the filing cabinet your company *should* have had for years — now searchable, and available to every proposal.

### 2.2 Procurements (your projects)

A **procurement** (also called a *project*) is one opportunity you are pursuing: one RFP, one customer, one deadline. Each procurement holds its own **documents** — the RFP, the statement of work, amendments, anything the customer sends.

### 2.3 Documents (the sources)

Each procurement has two groups of documents:

- **Requirement Documents** — the solicitation files (RFP, SOW, amendments) tied to *this* procurement.
- **Vault Documents** — company knowledge available across *all* procurements.

When you upload an RFP, Proposal Genie automatically starts **dissecting** it (see below). You can also add documents from URLs, and every document feeds the chat.

### 2.4 Chat (ask your documents)

The **Chat** tab lets you talk to your own documents. Ask "what is the page limit?" or "who is the contracting officer?" — and the answer comes with citations to the exact documents it used. It also knows the rules extracted by dissection: format, scoring, milestones, compliance. The model never has to guess, because everything it needs is loaded into the conversation.

### 2.5 Dissect RFP (the secret weapon)

**Dissection** is Proposal Genie reading the entire RFP for you and pulling out the four things you must know:

1. **Compliance requirements** — every "shall" and "must": forms, certifications, formatting, content mandates. These become a **compliance matrix** — a checklist that tracks every requirement as *addressed, partial, or missing*.
2. **Scoring** — every evaluation factor and its weight (e.g., Technical 50%, Past Performance 30%, Price 20%).
3. **Milestones** — the dates: questions due, site visits, proposal due, award target.
4. **Format** — font, font size, page limits per volume, margins, sections.

The result is saved as a **Solicitation Intelligence** document in your vault, and it is injected into every chat and proposal function, so the model *always* knows the rules.

### 2.6 The Proposal Studio (the writer's room)

The **Proposal Studio** is where you turn intelligence into a proposal. It has pre-set functions:

- **Generate the cover page** — the title page with your company and the customer's program name.
- **Integrate win themes** — your three or four core messages, repeated consistently through the proposal.
- **Analyze the competition** — who else is bidding and how to out-position them.
- **Write sections** — the technical and management narratives, one section at a time.
- **Assemble proposal** — combine everything into one document.

Every generated piece of markdown can be **saved to the vault**, so it becomes a source for the next round.

### 2.7 The Scout (finding opportunities)

The **Scout** is a search agent that watches **SAM.gov** and other sources for opportunities that fit your company. It learns over time: the more you tell it about your products, capabilities, and past performance — and the more outcomes you record — the better its searches become. This is a self-improving system.

### 2.8 Customers (the knowledge base that learns)

The **Customers** tab is a knowledge base about the agencies you sell to — MDA, DIU, Space Systems Command, NOAA, NASA, and so on. It learns automatically:

- When you **dissect** an RFP, the customer record gains mission, priorities, hot buttons, and buying patterns.
- When you record a **win or loss**, the win/loss counters update.
- You can build an **org chart** of the customer's leadership from public sources (Wikipedia, agency websites) — who the commander is, who the program offices are.

Every proposal becomes smarter because the next one starts with everything you learned about that customer.

### 2.9 The Office (making the documents)

The **Office** tab turns your markdown into finished files:

- **Word** — the proposal document, with your **logo in the header of every page**.
- **Excel** — data spreadsheets.
- **PowerPoint** — slide decks, including **presentation templates**:
  - **Quad Chart** — the classic four-box government chart (Technical / Management / Past Performance / Price).
  - **Gantt Chart** — the project schedule with task bars and milestones.
  - **Staffing Report** — who's on the team and what they do.
  - **Standard Deck** — your normal slide presentation.

### 2.10 The loop

Here is the whole point: **sources in → chat → generate → save → become sources.** The proposal you write today is the past performance you cite tomorrow. Proposal Genie closes the loop so nothing is ever lost.

## Part 3 — The NLP Way: Write Like the Customer

This is the heart of the book, and it is the heart of Proposal Genie's writing method.

### 3.1 What NLP actually is

**NLP** stands for **Neuro-Linguistic Programming**. (You may also see it written *Neurolinguistic Programming*.) It was developed in the 1970s as a way of studying how successful people communicate — how the mind (*neuro*), language (*linguistic*), and behavior (*programming*) work together.

You do not need a degree in it. For this book, NLP gives us one practical truth:

> **People trust people who sound like them.**

When someone uses your words, mirrors your tone, and reflects your priorities back to you, you feel *understood*. You relax. You listen. This is called **rapport** — a feeling of connection — and in NLP it is not luck. It is a skill you can practice.

### 3.2 The three NLP tools you will use

**1. Mirroring — repeat the customer's words.**

This is the simplest and most powerful technique in this book. The customer gives you their vocabulary in the RFP, their mission statement, their website, their past awards. **Use their exact words.** If they say "resilient," don't write "robust." If they say "integrated early warning," don't write "unified alert system."

Why does this work? Because evaluators read dozens of proposals. Yours is the one that sounds like *their* program. It feels written for them — because it was.

**2. Pacing and leading — agree first, then guide.**

NLP says: before you take someone somewhere new, first stand where they are. In a proposal, *pacing* means opening by reflecting the customer's reality — their mission, their problem, their words. *Leading* means then presenting your solution.

> **Pace:** "The agency must deliver resilient space capabilities in an era of growing threats."
> **Lead:** "Our approach builds that resilience by pairing proven launch heritage with a modern, modular ground architecture."

The evaluator reads the first sentence and nods — *they* understand us. Then they are open to your second sentence.

**3. Sensory language — match how they think.**

NLP observes that people favor different kinds of language:

- **Visual** people say: *I see what you mean. It looks right. Show me.*
- **Auditory** people say: *That sounds right. Let's talk about it. I hear you.*
- **Kinesthetic** (feeling) people say: *It feels right. Let's get a grip on it. Solid.*

Agencies and programs have their own style. Defense and space programs often favor *kinesthetic* words: *robust, resilient, hardened, agile, force* — words of weight and action. Science agencies often favor *visual* language: *observe, illuminate, look, horizon*. Read your customer's documents and notice the flavor. Then write in it.

### 3.3 Where to harvest the customer's words

Mirroring needs raw material. Proposal Genie collects it for you:

- **The RFP itself** (via dissection) — every phrase they use for the program, the mission, the requirements.
- **The customer's mission statement** — often in the RFP's opening pages, and on their website.
- **The customer's own documents** — their strategy documents, their solicitations, their speeches.
- **Your customer knowledge base** — hot buttons and priorities you have learned from past procurements.

### 3.4 Before and after: mirroring in action

**Before (generic — could be any company, any customer):**

> "Our company has extensive experience in satellite systems and will provide a robust solution for your requirements. We have a strong team with many years of experience."

Read that as an evaluator. It tells you *nothing* about them. It could be pasted into any proposal on Earth. It uses none of the customer's words.

**After (mirrored — written for this customer):**

> "The Space Systems Command requires resilient, survivable space capabilities that keep the joint force connected. Our team has delivered **on-orbit resiliency** across 12 national security missions, and our approach is built around the **portfolio management** model SSC uses to field capabilities at the speed of relevance — including a dedicated program executive office structure, an integrated master schedule, and a technical approach that **protects the mission through all phases of threat**."

The second version borrows the customer's vocabulary (*resilient, survivable, portfolio management, at the speed of relevance, program executive office*) and reflects their structure back at them. Same company. Same facts. Completely different feeling.

### 3.5 The rules of mirroring

- **Mirror facts, not claims.** Use their words, but never invent experience to match them. If you cannot back a claim, flag it as a gap — the soul of Proposal Genie forbids inflating claims.
- **Mirror, don't copy.** Paraphrase into your voice while keeping their vocabulary. Do not lift whole sentences from the RFP.
- **Mirror the mission, not the RFP.** The RFP is the rules; the customer is the audience. Mirror how they talk about *their mission and success*, not just the instructions.
- **Be specific.** If a sentence could appear in any company's proposal, rewrite it. Specificity *is* the mirror — it shows you actually read them.
- **Pace before you lead.** Always let the evaluator hear their own reality first.

### 3.6 One more NLP idea: the meaning of communication is the response you get

NLP has a famous principle: *the meaning of your communication is the response you get.* If the evaluator doesn't understand, doesn't feel addressed, doesn't see their words — then the communication failed, no matter how good you think it is.

This is why Proposal Genie **closes the loop**: after a draft is written, it checks the draft against the compliance matrix and the scoring criteria. *Addressed, partial, missing.* It verifies the proposal actually responds to what the customer asked — because a beautiful proposal that misses a "shall" is eliminated, and that is a personal failure in this way of working.

## Part 4 — The Complete Glossary

Every term used in this project, explained in plain English. (Terms already defined in Part 1 are summarized here for quick reference.)

### A–C
- **8(a) Program** — an SBA program that helps small disadvantaged businesses win government contracts, including sole-source awards.
- **Amendment** — a change the agency makes to a solicitation after it is published. Always read them; they change the rules.
- **Best Value** — a scoring approach where the agency weighs technical merit, past performance, and price together — it can pay more for a stronger proposal.
- **Bid** — an offer to do the work, at a price. (See also *proposal*.)
- **Brand (in Proposal Genie)** — one company profile in the Corporate Vault, with its own documents, voice, and procurements.
- **Brand Voice** — the way your company talks; a file that tells the AI how to write in your company's style. (Renamed "Company" in the Office tab.)
- **Capture** — the early phase of winning: research, relationships, shaping the requirement — before the proposal is even written.
- **Capture Document** — a living summary of an opportunity: scope, risks, evaluation criteria, win themes, and who's who.
- **Chat (in Proposal Genie)** — talk to your own documents; answers come with citations.
- **Compliance** — following every rule in the solicitation: every "shall," every form, every formatting rule.
- **Compliance Matrix** — a checklist mapping every requirement to where in your proposal it is addressed (addressed / partial / missing).
- **Contracting Officer (CO)** — the only person legally authorized to sign the contract.
- **Corporate Vault** — the company's private knowledge base: past proposals, product sheets, capability statements, brand voice.
- **Cover Page** — the title page of the proposal: program name, company, solicitation number, date.

### D–F
- **Debrief** — after an award, losers can get a meeting explaining why they won or lost. Invaluable for learning.
- **Dissection / Dissect RFP** — Proposal Genie reading an RFP and extracting compliance requirements, scoring, milestones, and format rules.
- **Document (in Proposal Genie)** — any file in the vault: RFP files, company files, generated proposals.
- **Evaluation Factors** — the criteria in Section M that the agency scores. Know them, in order, by weight.
- **Experts** — configurable personas (e.g., project manager, business development, proposal writer, subject matter expert) that can be set in the chat.
- **FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation)** — the federal rulebook for buying. Subpart 15.2 governs RFPs.
- **Format Guide** — the extracted rules for how the proposal must look: font, size, margins, page limits, volumes.
- **FPDS** — the Federal Procurement Data System: the database of federal contract awards over $25,000.

### G–L
- **Gantt Chart** — a schedule drawn as horizontal bars: tasks, durations, milestones. The backbone of your management plan.
- **Go/No-Go** — the early decision: do we bid or not? Based on fit, capacity, competition, and win probability.
- **GSA Schedule** — a pre-negotiated catalog contract with the General Services Administration; getting on it makes selling easier.
- **HUBZone** — a set-aside program for businesses in historically underutilized business zones.
- **IFB (Invitation for Bid)** — a solicitation where the lowest acceptable price wins (sealed bid).
- **Intelligence (Solicitation Intelligence)** — the distilled knowledge from dissection: compliance, scoring, milestones, format.
- **LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable)** — a scoring approach: cheapest proposal that meets the minimum technical bar wins.
- **Logo Header** — your company logo printed in the header of every page of a generated Word document.

### M–O
- **Management Volume** — the proposal section describing how you will run the work: team, organization, schedule, staffing.
- **Milestones** — key dates in the procurement: questions due, site visit, proposal due, award target.
- **Mirroring (NLP)** — repeating the customer's words and priorities back to them so they feel understood.
- **NAICS Code** — the government's industry classification number (e.g., 541511 for custom programming). Used to search and to classify your business.
- **NLP (Neuro-Linguistic Programming)** — the study of how language and mindset shape communication; our method for writing like the customer.
- **Office (tab)** — converts markdown into Word, Excel, and PowerPoint, including quad charts, Gantt charts, and staffing reports.
- **Org Chart (Customer)** — the leadership structure of the agency you're selling to, researched from public sources.
- **Offeror** — a company that submits a proposal. That's you.

### P–R
- **Pacing and Leading (NLP)** — agreeing with the customer's reality first (pace), then presenting your solution (lead).
- **Past Performance** — your history of doing similar work; written as evidence-backed stories with real outcomes.
- **Portfolio / Program** — an agency's collection of related projects; the level where funding and priorities are set.
- **Price/Cost Volume** — the proposal section with your pricing and supporting rationale.
- **Procurement** — one opportunity you are pursuing (a "project" in Proposal Genie): one RFP, one customer, one deadline.
- **Proposal** — your complete offer: technical approach, management plan, past performance, price.
- **Proposal Studio** — the Proposal Genie tab with the pre-set functions: cover page, win themes, competition analysis, sections, assembly.
- **Protest** — a formal challenge to an award (typically filed within 10 days with the GAO) if the rules were broken.
- **Quad Chart** — the four-box chart (Technical / Management / Past Performance / Price) used in many federal briefs.
- **Rapport (NLP)** — a feeling of connection and trust; built deliberately by mirroring language.
- **RFI / RFQ / RFP** — Request for Information / Quotation / Proposal (see Part 1.3).

### S
- **SAM.gov** — the federal system: register your business here, and search all contract opportunities over $25,000.
- **Scoring** — how the agency grades proposals, per Section M: criteria, weights, and the selection framework (best value or LPTA).
- **Scout** — the self-improving opportunity finder that watches SAM.gov and other sources for work that fits your company.
- **SDVOSB** — Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business; a set-aside category.
- **Section L / Section M** — the RFP's instructions (L) and evaluation factors (M). The two pages that matter most.
- **Set-Aside** — a contract reserved for small businesses (or a category like 8(a), HUBZone, WOSB, SDVOSB).
- **"Shall"** — the strongest word in the RFP. Every "shall" is a requirement. Miss one and you can be eliminated.
- **Sole-Source** — a contract awarded without competition, usually because only one company can do it.
- **Solicitation** — the official announcement asking companies to compete (an RFP, RFQ, or IFB).
- **SOW (Statement of Work)** — the customer's detailed description of the work to be done.
- **Staffing Report** — the team plan: who fills each role, what they bring, how the team is organized.
- **Subject Matter Expert (SME)** — an expert in a specific technical or domain area.

### T–W
- **Technical Volume** — the proposal section describing your approach, your solution, your understanding of the problem.
- **Volumes** — the separately bound parts of a proposal: Technical, Management, Past Performance, Cost/Price.
- **Win Themes** — your three or four core messages, repeated consistently: what makes you different, why the customer should believe you, and why you'll succeed.
- **WOSB** — Women-Owned Small Business; a set-aside category.

## Part 5 — A Beginner's Walkthrough

Let's put it all together with a simple example. A small company ("Acme Aerospace") finds an RFP on SAM.gov from the **U.S. Space Force** for a ground systems modernization effort.

**Step 1 — Scout.** Acme's Scout finds the opportunity because Acme's profile mentions satellite ground systems. Acme opens it in the **Procurements** tab and uploads the RFP.

**Step 2 — Go/No-Go.** Acme reads the RFP summary. The work matches their capabilities, they have a team, and the competition looks manageable. **Go.** (They record this in their capture notes.)

**Step 3 — Dissect.** Acme clicks **Dissect RFP**. Proposal Genie extracts:
- *Compliance:* 9 "shall" requirements, including a required form and a 12-page limit.
- *Scoring:* Technical 50%, Management 30%, Price 20%.
- *Milestones:* questions due in 2 weeks, proposal due in 6 weeks.
- *Format:* Times New Roman 12pt, 1-inch margins, page numbers in footer.

Acme now has a **compliance matrix** and knows the rules — before writing a word.

**Step 4 — Harvest the customer's words.** Acme reads the RFP's mission language and the Space Force's mission statement, and builds the win themes around *their* words: "resilient," "survivable," "at the speed of relevance," "portfolio management."

**Step 5 — Write.** Acme uses the **Proposal Studio** to generate the cover page, then writes sections the NLP way — pacing with the customer's mission, leading with Acme's approach, mirroring their vocabulary, citing specific past performance (no invented claims).

**Step 6 — Verify.** Before submitting, Proposal Genie checks the draft against the compliance matrix: addressed, partial, missing. Every "shall" is covered. Format rules are checked. Page limit is respected.

**Step 7 — Deliver.** Acme exports the proposal to **Word** with their **logo in the header**, plus a **Gantt chart** and **staffing report** for the management volume, and a **quad chart** for the oral brief.

**Step 8 — Learn.** Win or lose, Acme records the outcome. The **Customers** knowledge base grows: hot buttons, lessons, contacts. The next proposal — for the same customer or a new one — starts smarter.

That is the whole method: **find it, understand it, write like them, verify everything, learn forever.**

## Final Word

Winning government work is not magic. It is a repeatable process:

1. **Understand the rules** (compliance, scoring, format) — Proposal Genie's dissection.
2. **Understand the customer** (mission, words, priorities) — the Customers knowledge base and the NLP way.
3. **Write like them** — mirror their words, pace their reality, lead with your solution.
4. **Verify everything** — compliance is non-negotiable.
5. **Learn from every outcome** — the debrief is not the end; it is the beginning of the next proposal.

And the single most practical sentence in this entire book:

> **Repeat the customer's words back to them.**

Write like that, and you won't just be another bidder — you'll be the one who understood them.
